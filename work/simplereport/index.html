SimpleReport

Helping health workers send rapid test results to the CDC

My role

I was the senior product designer on SimpleReport at Skylight. I led design for the workflows clinic staff use to report results and manage their testing sites, and I scaled our design system, built on USWDS (the U.S. Web Design System), working closely with engineering.

The problem

Testing sites waited about two days on support before they could test for a new condition.

SimpleReport started as a COVID-19 reporting tool. When testing sites wanted to report on other conditions, like flu or RSV, they couldn’t turn them on themselves. They had to contact support and wait, often about two days, before they could start testing.

I mapped the experience across the three user roles, from setup through registering patients, running tests and managing patient info. Setup was the biggest blocker: I don’t like that I need to wait on customer support.

The hypothesis

If admins could turn on new conditions themselves, the CDC would get more results, for more diseases.

$32M was invested to expand SimpleReport beyond COVID-19. The hypothesis was that if health admins could quickly enable additional diseases, the CDC would receive a higher volume and range of test results.

I defined success as:

  • Less time to enable a new condition
  • Fewer support tickets
  • More adoption beyond COVID-19

The users

Designed for two very different users: nurses who use it all shift, and admins who use it now and then.

Nurses use SimpleReport repeatedly over a full shift. Turnover is high, so there’s no time for training. Speed and clarity matter above everything.

Health administrators manage settings for the entire site. They’re time-poor and juggle multiple responsibilities. They use the tool only occasionally, but they’re the decision-makers.

In SimpleReport, administrators are org admins: the only role that can change what a site tests for. Nurses work as standard or entry-only users.

Constraints

Each constraint shaped the design.

  • A growing device list → a design that scales. Every new test device added to the catalog made the list longer, so the design couldn’t depend on its length.
  • User permissions → control over who changes settings. Three roles (org admin, standard and entry-only) meant controlling who could change site settings. Only org admins can change what a site tests for. Standard and entry-only users can’t.
  • Self-serve → no waiting on support. Admins needed to make changes on their own, without contacting support.

Explorations

Three explorations, each showing less of the catalog: everything → filtered → only what the site runs.

Exploration one: a toggle list

Familiar, but it grew with every device.

Every device in the catalog had an on/off toggle. Admins knew how to use it, but every new device made the list longer.

Exploration two: search and filters

Faster to find a device, but the whole catalog still lived on the page.

Search and condition filters made it faster to find the right device. But the full catalog was still on the page, and the list would keep growing.

Exploration three: show what the site runs (selected)

Show only the devices the site uses, and keep the catalog one step away.

The page shows only the devices the site actually uses, and the full catalog stays one step away, behind Add devices. Validated with 5 admins.

Results

Sites turned on new conditions in minutes instead of days, and reporting beyond COVID-19 more than quadrupled.

  • 2 days → minutes to enable a condition
  • 75% fewer support tickets
  • 12% → 52% of reporting beyond COVID-19

Reflection

The best proof a design works is the workaround disappearing.

Before, turning on a new condition meant a support ticket and a two-day wait. Once admins could do it themselves, support tickets dropped by 75%. The workaround didn’t need to be discouraged; it simply stopped being necessary.
